# 26 — Content Outline and Information Architecture

## 1. Role of this file

This file is the authoritative page-level outline and information-architecture reference for the Neetrino SEO Blog GPT. Use it after a content brief has established the audience, intent, page role, reader promise, scope, evidence plan, conversion path, and canonical ownership, and before full drafting or detailed design begins.

It defines:

- how to turn an approved brief into a coherent sequence of reader decisions;
- how to distinguish site architecture, page architecture, outline, heading hierarchy, section contract, and visual layout;
- how to expose the primary answer early without flattening a complex subject;
- how to order definitions, explanations, evidence, examples, comparisons, risks, and actions;
- how to support scanning, deep reading, mobile use, accessibility, and answer extraction;
- how to handle mixed intent, multiple reader states, technical depth, and conditional paths;
- how to decide whether information belongs in a section, component, separate page, appendix, or not at all;
- how to prevent keyword-shaped outlines, duplicate sections, false hierarchy, and unnecessary length;
- how to document section-level purpose, claims, evidence, format, links, and transitions;
- how to validate an outline before drafting and a rendered page before publication.

This file does not select topics, validate demand, assign cluster ownership, create the original content brief, write final copy, or define the entire website navigation. Those decisions belong to their respective canonical files.

## 2. Core operating principle

An outline is a model of how a reader moves from their current state to a useful outcome. It is not a list of keywords, a transcript of research notes, or a collection of plausible H2s.

Use this chain:

> reader state → immediate orientation → primary answer → necessary understanding → credible evidence → decision or action support → appropriate next step

Include each section because it performs a distinct job in that chain. Remove, merge, move, or externalize any section that does not materially improve comprehension, trust, decision quality, task completion, or navigation.

## 3. Required conceptual distinctions

### 3.1 Site information architecture

The organization of pages, navigation systems, URLs, taxonomies, hubs, clusters, and cross-page relationships across the website. It determines where a page belongs and how users can reach connected assets.

### 3.2 Page information architecture

The hierarchy, order, grouping, labeling, and disclosure of information within one page. It determines how the page fulfils its promise.

### 3.3 Content outline

The planned sequence of sections and components required to move the primary reader through the task. A useful outline records why each section exists, not only its proposed heading.

### 3.4 Heading hierarchy

The semantic nesting of H1–H6 labels that communicates relationships among sections. Heading levels express structure; they are not font-size controls.

### 3.5 Section contract

The smallest planning unit that states a section's reader question, job, required answer, supporting evidence, preferred format, boundaries, links, and transition.

### 3.6 Content component

A structured presentation unit such as a table, callout, checklist, steps block, quote, diagram, calculator, code sample, FAQ, or CTA. A component is selected for a task, not decoration.

### 3.7 Rendered layout

The visual implementation of the approved architecture across devices. Design may improve hierarchy and usability but must not silently change content ownership, meaning, order, or accessibility.

### 3.8 Reader path

The intended progression through the page. It may be mostly linear, selectively scannable, or branched by role or condition, but it must remain understandable without requiring every optional branch.

### 3.9 Information scent

The cues in headings, labels, summaries, links, and navigation that help a reader predict what they will find next. Clever wording must not weaken those cues.

### 3.10 Progressive disclosure

The practice of showing the information required for the current decision first and making secondary detail available when needed. It is not permission to hide essential caveats or evidence.

### 3.11 Answer unit

A concise, self-contained passage that answers one meaningful question accurately enough to be understood in context and extracted without changing its meaning.

### 3.12 Structural completeness

The condition in which the page covers every requirement necessary to fulfil its promise, while excluding information that belongs elsewhere. Completeness is not maximal length.

## 4. Authority and dependency order

When structural instructions conflict, apply this order:

1. the user's explicit current request and approved scope;
2. truth, safety, privacy, legal, accessibility, and approval requirements;
3. canonical Neetrino brand, service, audience, proof, and factual-owner files;
4. approved intent, keyword, SERP, gap, cluster, and page-ownership records;
5. the approved content brief and page specification;
6. this file for page-level structure and sequence;
7. writing, conversion, on-page SEO, design, development, and publishing systems;
8. stylistic preferences that do not weaken clarity or integrity.

Do not use outline work to reopen approved strategy silently. If the promise cannot be fulfilled within the approved page role or evidence, return the conflict to the brief owner.

## 5. Minimum inputs before outlining

Gather or explicitly label as unknown:

- primary reader, context, knowledge level, trigger, task, and desired outcome;
- primary intent, secondary compatible intents, and journey stage;
- page role, page action, canonical owner, and cluster relationships;
- page promise, core thesis, and primary answer;
- must, should, may, and must-not-cover scope;
- material objections, risks, constraints, and decision criteria;
- approved claims, source requirements, evidence availability, and freshness needs;
- required internal links, inbound-link expectations, and CTA readiness;
- required assets, interactions, structured data, localization, and accessibility needs;
- existing-page constraints when updating, merging, splitting, or repositioning.

If the primary task, promise, ownership, or evidence feasibility is unresolved, create only a provisional outline and label the blockers.

## 6. Outline lifecycle and states

Assign one explicit state:

| State | Meaning | Allowed action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs incomplete | A structural dependency is missing | Resolve or document assumption |
| Skeleton | Major reader stages are mapped | Test sequence and scope |
| Section planning | Contracts and formats are being assigned | Research and editorial review |
| Ready for review | Full architecture can be evaluated | Stakeholder review |
| Approved | Structure and dependencies are accepted | Begin drafting/design |
| Revision required | Evidence, scope, or implementation changed materially | Update outline first |
| In production | Drafting or implementation follows the outline | Track structural deviations |
| Ready for structural QA | Draft/render exists | Compare delivery to contracts |
| Published | Final structure and URL are recorded | Monitor behavior and maintain |
| Superseded | Another approved structure replaces it | Preserve traceability; stop use |

An outline is not approved merely because every heading has text beneath it.

## 7. Choose the dominant page pattern

Select the pattern that best matches the reader task. Hybrid pages are allowed, but one pattern must govern the opening and primary path.

| Pattern | Best for | Typical sequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct answer | Focused informational question | Answer → context → evidence → implications |
| Diagnostic | Identifying a problem or maturity state | Symptoms → criteria → diagnosis → response |
| How-to | Completing a task | Outcome → prerequisites → steps → validation → recovery |
| Requirements | Planning a solution | Goal → requirements → decisions → risks → checklist |
| Comparison | Choosing among alternatives | Decision frame → criteria → comparison → fit → verdict |
| Evaluation | Assessing a vendor, system, or approach | Context → criteria → evidence → risks → next step |
| Pillar | Understanding a broad decision domain | Orientation → map → key branches → child routes |
| Hub | Finding the right resource | Purpose → grouped paths → labels → next destinations |
| Case study | Evaluating demonstrated capability | Context → challenge → intervention → evidence → lessons |
| Service | Determining fit and initiating action | Problem → outcome → approach → proof → fit → CTA |
| Technical reference | Retrieving exact implementation information | Summary → prerequisites → specification → examples → edge cases |
| Tool or template | Producing an output | Use case → inputs → tool → interpretation → next action |

Do not force every page into introduction–benefits–steps–conclusion. Familiarity is useful only when it reflects the task.

## 8. Build the reader-state map first

Before writing headings, record:

1. what the reader likely knows;
2. what they may believe incorrectly;
3. what they need answered immediately;
4. what they must understand before trusting or acting;
5. what evidence changes their confidence;
6. what choice, task, or next step the page should enable;
7. what adjacent needs belong on other pages.

Translate this into no more than seven major reader stages. If the path requires many independent stages, reassess page scope or introduce clear navigation.

## 9. Opening architecture

### 9.1 Opening contract

The opening should establish, in an order appropriate to risk:

- what the page addresses;
- who and what context it applies to;
- the primary answer, outcome, or decision frame;
- any essential qualification that prevents a misleading interpretation;
- what the reader will be able to do next.

### 9.2 Answer-first rule

For a focused informational query, provide the shortest accurate answer before history, methodology, brand narrative, or generic scene-setting. For high-stakes, conditional, or technical topics, state the answer with its decisive conditions.

Do not delay a useful answer to manufacture engagement. Confusion is not suspense.

### 9.3 When not to force a one-sentence answer

Use a concise decision frame instead when:

- the answer depends materially on context;
- several options are valid for distinct conditions;
- a binary answer would be unsafe or false;
- the page is a hub or tool rather than an explanatory article;
- evidence must be introduced before a conclusion can be interpreted responsibly.

### 9.4 Introduction limits

Remove opening material that only repeats the title, defines an obvious term, announces that the topic is important, or describes what “this article will discuss” without helping the reader orient.

## 10. Section sequencing logic

Order sections by dependency, not by keyword value or research discovery order.

For each proposed section, ask:

1. What question or decision does it resolve?
2. What prior information does it require?
3. What later section depends on it?
4. Can it be understood if reached directly from search or a table of contents?
5. Does moving it earlier reduce uncertainty?
6. Does moving it later prevent overload?
7. Is it essential, supporting, optional, or out of scope?

Use these common relationships:

- **definition before use** when the term is unfamiliar or ambiguous;
- **answer before elaboration** for focused queries;
- **criteria before comparison** so the verdict is auditable;
- **prerequisites before steps** when omission creates failure;
- **risk before commitment** when the risk changes the choice;
- **evidence near the claim** rather than in a disconnected proof section;
- **example after explanation** unless the example is the clearest entry point;
- **decision summary before CTA** so action follows understanding.

## 11. Scope-to-structure mapping

Map every approved scope item to one destination:

| Scope class | Structural treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Must cover | Dedicated section or explicit part of a section contract |
| Should cover | Include when it improves promise completion without displacing essentials |
| May cover | Use only if evidence, length, and reader value justify it |
| Must not cover | Exclude or route to the canonical owner |
| Unknown | Flag for brief clarification; do not invent silently |

Maintain a coverage matrix. Every must-cover item requires an owner; every outline section requires a justification. This catches both omissions and ornamental sections.

## 12. Section-versus-page decision

Keep a subject as a section when it:

- serves the same primary reader and task;
- shares the page's promise and next action;
- can be answered proportionally without dominating the page;
- requires no materially different evidence model or format;
- does not already have a stronger canonical owner.

Create or route to a separate page when it:

- represents a distinct primary intent or reader task;
- requires substantial depth, maintenance, or specialist review;
- targets a different journey stage or CTA readiness;
- needs an interactive, technical, local, or role-specific experience;
- would make the parent page lose focus;
- already belongs to another page owner.

Use a concise summary plus an internal link when the parent reader needs orientation but not full treatment.

## 13. Section contract

Every material section must record:

- working heading;
- reader question or decision;
- section job;
- required answer or takeaway;
- claims and evidence needed;
- preferred format or component;
- example, scenario, or data need;
- boundary and exclusions;
- relevant internal or external links;
- entry assumption and exit transition;
- priority: essential, supporting, or optional;
- owner or reviewer when specialized.

A heading without a contract invites repetition and filler during drafting.

## 14. Heading hierarchy

### 14.1 H1 rule

Use one clear H1 for the page's primary subject and promise. It may differ from the SEO title, but both must describe the same owner intent.

### 14.2 H2 rule

Use H2s for the major stages, questions, or decision units of the page. A reader scanning only the H2s should understand the page's route.

### 14.3 H3 and deeper levels

Use H3s for true subdivisions of an H2. Introduce H4–H6 only when the content genuinely requires deeper semantic nesting. If many deep levels are needed, consider a table, steps component, tabs, accordion, separate page, or simplified structure.

### 14.4 Heading labels

Prefer headings that communicate the section's answer, question, decision, or content type. Avoid:

- vague labels such as “Overview,” “More information,” or “Other”;
- repeated variants of the page title;
- clever metaphors that hide information scent;
- keyword permutations that do not represent distinct sections;
- heading fragments that depend entirely on the preceding paragraph;
- FAQ-style questions when the section is actually a substantial guide.

### 14.5 Semantic integrity

Do not skip levels for visual effect. Do not use bold paragraphs as substitute headings. Do not choose a heading level based on desired font size.

## 15. Paragraph, list, table, and component decisions

Choose the smallest format that preserves meaning.

| Reader task | Preferred starting format |
| --- | --- |
| Understand a concept | Short prose plus example |
| Follow a sequence | Numbered steps |
| Scan unordered factors | Bulleted list |
| Compare exact attributes | Table |
| Choose by conditions | Decision table or flow |
| Verify completion | Checklist |
| Interpret a metric | Chart plus prose explanation |
| Understand relationships | Diagram when prose is materially harder |
| Reuse a process | Template or downloadable artifact |
| Execute code | Tested code block plus prerequisites and result |
| Notice a critical caveat | Accessible callout near the affected content |

Do not convert nuanced arguments into lists merely for scanability. Do not use tables for long narrative cells that become unusable on mobile.

## 16. Progressive disclosure

Place information in three layers:

1. **Immediate** — answer, decisive conditions, key action, and essential warning.
2. **Supporting** — explanation, criteria, evidence, examples, and practical detail.
3. **Optional depth** — edge cases, methodology, advanced implementation, references, or related pages.

Use summaries, anchor navigation, accordions, tabs, expandable code, appendices, or linked child pages only when they preserve access and meaning.

Never hide:

- a material limitation that changes the conclusion;
- pricing or conditions required for a commercial claim when disclosure is expected;
- safety, privacy, legal, or implementation risks;
- the evidence basis for a disputed or consequential claim;
- information required to complete the primary task.

## 17. Mixed-intent and branched pages

Support secondary intent only when it is compatible with the primary promise.

### 17.1 Branching rules

- Establish the shared answer or frame first.
- Label branches by reader condition, role, use case, or decision—not by vague personas.
- Keep each branch internally coherent.
- Let readers identify the relevant branch quickly.
- Rejoin branches at a shared decision or next-step summary when appropriate.
- Route materially different tasks to separate owners.

### 17.2 Branch formats

Use:

- jump links for long but stable sections;
- decision tables for conditional choices;
- tabs only when all content remains accessible and indexable as required;
- accordions for optional detail, not core answers;
- separate pages when branches differ in intent, evidence, format, or CTA.

## 18. Evidence architecture

Evidence belongs where it helps the reader evaluate a claim.

For every material claim, record:

- claim type and owner;
- evidence type and source;
- required date or freshness;
- exact placement;
- whether context, method, sample, limitation, or uncertainty must accompany it;
- whether the evidence can be quoted, paraphrased, visualized, or linked;
- what to do if evidence is unavailable.

Avoid dumping citations at the end of a page without linking them to claims. Avoid separating every proof point into a generic “Why trust us” block when evidence can appear naturally in the relevant section.

## 19. Examples and scenarios

Use an example when it makes an abstraction concrete, reveals a trade-off, demonstrates a process, or helps the reader transfer knowledge.

Label clearly whether an example is:

- a verified Neetrino case;
- an anonymized real scenario;
- a sourced external example;
- a hypothetical illustration;
- a simplified calculation;
- an interface or code example.

Do not present a composite or hypothetical scenario as a client result. Place examples close to the principle they clarify.

## 20. Comparison and decision architecture

For a comparison or alternatives page, use this order unless evidence requires another:

1. decision context and eligible audience;
2. compared options and scope;
3. fair criteria with rationale;
4. concise comparison summary;
5. criterion-by-criterion evidence;
6. fit and non-fit by use case;
7. limitations, switching factors, and uncertainty;
8. qualified conclusion and next step.

State criteria before scoring. Apply the same standard to every option. Do not manufacture a universal winner when fit depends on conditions.

## 21. How-to and implementation architecture

Include:

- observable outcome;
- prerequisites, permissions, inputs, and version context;
- risks or irreversible actions before they occur;
- numbered steps with one meaningful action per step;
- expected result or verification after critical steps;
- recovery or troubleshooting for likely failures;
- completion criteria;
- maintenance or next action.

Do not bury prerequisites midway through the process. Separate optional branches from the main path.

## 22. Technical-reference architecture

Prioritize precision and retrieval speed. Include as relevant:

- scope, supported versions, environment, and assumptions;
- concise behavior summary;
- exact requirements or interface specification;
- tested examples with inputs and expected outputs;
- edge cases, limits, security implications, and failure modes;
- validation and troubleshooting;
- update date and technical owner.

Keep conceptual explanation available, but do not force an experienced reader through it before reaching exact reference information.

## 23. Service-page architecture

A service page should help a qualified reader determine relevance and confidence, not merely praise the company.

Structure around:

- recognizable problem or trigger;
- intended business outcome;
- scope and deliverables;
- process and collaboration expectations;
- evidence and differentiation;
- integrations, constraints, risks, or non-fit conditions;
- engagement or next-step options;
- clear CTA appropriate to readiness.

Integrate proof beside the promise it supports. Avoid repeating one generic CTA after every section.

## 24. Case-study architecture

Preserve causal honesty:

1. client or anonymized context;
2. challenge and baseline;
3. constraints and goals;
4. diagnosis or decision rationale;
5. intervention and Neetrino's actual role;
6. implementation and material changes;
7. measured results with timeframe and attribution limits;
8. lessons, transferability, and remaining limitations;
9. relevant next step.

Do not make the outcome the only visible substance. Readers need enough method and context to judge relevance.

## 25. FAQ architecture

Use FAQs for meaningful residual questions that do not deserve full sections and that support the primary task.

Each answer must:

- respond directly in the first sentence;
- remain accurate outside the surrounding sequence;
- include material conditions or limitations;
- avoid duplicating an earlier section;
- link to a canonical owner when deeper treatment exists.

Do not create FAQs solely to place keyword variants or qualify for structured data. A frequently asked question requires evidence of relevance, not imagination with a question mark.

## 26. Table of contents and in-page navigation

Add in-page navigation when page length, branching, or retrieval needs justify it. It should:

- reflect the actual semantic hierarchy;
- use recognizable labels;
- link to stable, unique anchors;
- avoid listing trivial subsections;
- remain usable by keyboard and assistive technology;
- work on mobile without dominating the viewport.

A table of contents does not repair a poorly sequenced page.

## 27. Internal-link placement within the page

Place internal links where they help the reader continue a task or verify context.

For each link, record:

- source section;
- destination owner and URL status;
- relationship: prerequisite, definition, evidence, child detail, next step, proof, service, or related path;
- intended reader action;
- natural anchor concept;
- fallback if the destination is not yet published.

Avoid unexplained “read more” links, excessive repeated destinations, and links inserted only because a keyword appears.

## 28. Conversion path within the architecture

Match conversion opportunities to reader readiness:

- early-stage pages may offer a related guide, checklist, diagnostic, or newsletter;
- evaluation pages may offer a requirements review, case study, estimate input, or consultation;
- service pages may offer direct contact or scoping;
- technical pages may route to implementation support only after completing the technical task.

Place the primary CTA after enough value and confidence exist. Use contextual micro-CTAs only where they offer a legitimate alternate path. The page must remain useful without conversion.

## 29. Search and answer-extraction readiness

Structure for human understanding first, while making accurate answers easy to identify:

- use descriptive headings aligned with real questions or decisions;
- place concise answers close to their headings;
- define entities and relationships unambiguously;
- state units, dates, markets, versions, and conditions;
- use lists, steps, and tables only when semantically appropriate;
- keep citations near claims;
- make page ownership and update date clear where freshness matters;
- avoid pronouns or fragments that make extracted passages misleading;
- include uncertainty and limitations inside the answer unit when material.

Do not create repetitive “snippet bait.” Extractability never overrides nuance or truth.

## 30. Mobile, scanning, and cognitive-load rules

Plan for narrow screens and interrupted reading:

- front-load section meaning;
- keep paragraphs focused on one idea;
- break long procedures into meaningful steps;
- avoid overly wide tables or provide an accessible alternative;
- repeat essential context when a section may be entered directly, without duplicating whole explanations;
- use summaries at genuine decision points;
- avoid stacked callouts, banners, sticky controls, and CTAs that obscure the content;
- ensure headings alone provide a useful scan path.

Short is not automatically clear. Reduce cognitive load through grouping, order, and labeling—not through removal of necessary conditions.

## 31. Accessibility requirements

The architecture must support:

- logical heading order;
- meaningful link labels;
- keyboard-accessible navigation and interactive components;
- text alternatives for informative visuals;
- captions, transcripts, or equivalent access for media as required;
- tables with clear headers and simple structures;
- instructions that do not depend only on color, position, shape, or sensory cues;
- visible focus and adequate reading order in the rendered page;
- no essential information hidden behind inaccessible interaction.

Accessibility is an architectural input, not a post-publication patch.

## 32. Multilingual information architecture

For Armenian, English, and Russian versions:

- preserve equivalent intent and page role, not necessarily identical section order;
- use natural local labels and audience terminology;
- adapt examples, objections, laws, tools, currencies, and market assumptions when material;
- re-evaluate answer length and heading rhythm for the language;
- preserve evidence meaning and citation integrity;
- map equivalent internal links explicitly;
- avoid adding a section in one language merely because a literal translation created awkward gaps;
- document intentional structural divergence.

Localization may change architecture when reader knowledge, market context, or available evidence differs.

## 33. Update-page architecture

Before changing an existing page:

1. map current sections, purpose, traffic-bearing queries, links, and evidence;
2. identify retained, revised, moved, merged, added, and removed sections;
3. preserve useful anchors when possible;
4. assess whether structural changes alter intent ownership;
5. protect valuable inbound and internal-link destinations;
6. update stale evidence and dependent summaries;
7. document redirects or child-page changes where applicable;
8. compare the final page against the new promise, not only the old outline.

Do not preserve weak sections solely because they already exist.

## 34. Merge and split architecture

### 34.1 Merge

Define:

- surviving canonical owner;
- unique material from each source;
- new unified reader path;
- duplicate or conflicting claims;
- links, anchors, metadata, and redirects to preserve;
- sections to retire rather than carry forward.

### 34.2 Split

Define:

- distinct intent owner for each new page;
- parent–child or sibling relationship;
- which content moves, stays, or is rewritten;
- cross-links and navigation;
- overlap boundaries;
- migration and measurement plan.

A split is justified by distinct reader tasks, not merely by page length.

## 35. Design and development handoff

For every non-trivial component, specify:

- content job and priority;
- required fields and data source;
- semantic element or accessibility behavior;
- desktop and mobile behavior;
- empty, loading, error, and long-content states where applicable;
- interaction and fallback behavior;
- tracking requirement if approved;
- CMS authoring constraints;
- owner and acceptance criteria.

Design may propose a better presentation, but any change to order, visibility, labels, evidence, or CTA must be reviewed against the section contracts.

## 36. Structural measurement plan

Choose measures related to the page task, such as:

- search-query and landing-page alignment;
- engaged reading or section visibility interpreted cautiously;
- table-of-contents or branch usage;
- internal-link continuation;
- tool or template completion;
- CTA interaction by reader stage;
- on-page search or repeated support questions;
- conversion quality rather than clicks alone;
- accessibility or usability findings;
- cannibalization, decay, or answer mismatch.

Do not infer comprehension from scroll depth alone. Define what evidence would justify reordering, expanding, separating, or removing a section.

## 38. Section coverage matrix

Maintain a compact matrix for standard and extended pages:

| Requirement | Section owner | Evidence | Format | Priority | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Page promise | Opening + named sections | Approved brief | Direct answer | Essential | Planned |
| Must-cover item | Exact section | Required source | Best-fit format | Essential | Planned |
| Objection/risk | Exact section | Evidence or expert review | Prose/callout/table | Essential or supporting | Planned |
| Internal destination | Source section | Canonical page | Contextual link | Supporting | Planned |
| CTA readiness | Decision section | Journey logic | CTA | Supporting | Planned |

No must-cover item may remain ownerless. No section should exist without a mapped requirement or justified reader need.

## 39. Outline quality scoring

Score each dimension from 0 to 2:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reader-task alignment | Unclear | Partial | Explicit throughout |
| Promise completion | Not demonstrated | Plausible | Fully mapped |
| Sequence | Arbitrary | Mostly logical | Dependency-led |
| Scope discipline | Bloated/missing | Minor issues | Complete and bounded |
| Section distinctness | Repetitive | Some overlap | Clear contracts |
| Answer accessibility | Delayed/hidden | Mixed | Immediate and qualified |
| Evidence placement | Absent/dumped | Partly mapped | Claim-level architecture |
| Heading hierarchy | Misleading | Mostly correct | Semantic and scannable |
| Format fitness | Decorative | Generally useful | Task-led components |
| Links and continuation | Random/absent | Partial | Intentional paths |
| Conversion integrity | Forced/absent | Acceptable | Readiness-matched |
| Accessibility/mobile | Ignored | Basic | Built into structure |
| Localization readiness | Literal/unknown | Partly considered | Market-aware mapping |
| Production clarity | Unowned | Some dependencies | Actionable handoff |

Interpretation:

- **24–28** — structurally ready, subject to required approvals;
- **18–23** — revise identified weaknesses before drafting;
- **0–17** — return to the brief, scope, or reader path.

A score cannot override a material truth, ownership, evidence, safety, or accessibility blocker.

## 40. Common anti-patterns

Reject or revise:

- keyword dumps converted into H2s;
- competitor heading structures copied with minor rewording;
- generic introductions that delay the answer;
- chronology when the reader needs a decision sequence;
- one section per keyword variant;
- headings that do not reveal content;
- multiple sections performing the same explanatory job;
- essential caveats isolated far from the claim;
- proof collected into an unrelated promotional block;
- FAQs duplicating the body;
- tables used where cells require essays;
- accordions hiding primary answers;
- deep heading levels created for visual styling;
- a separate page for every minor subquestion;
- a single page forced to own incompatible intents;
- arbitrary word-count targets controlling architecture;
- CTAs inserted at fixed intervals regardless of readiness;
- visuals planned without a reader task;
- mobile and accessibility deferred until implementation;
- literal multilingual duplication despite different market needs;
- outlines so prescriptive that they prevent expert improvement;
- outlines so vague that writers must remake the strategy.

## 41. Pre-draft structural checklist

Confirm:

- [ ] The approved brief and page owner are identified.
- [ ] The primary reader, task, promise, and answer are explicit.
- [ ] One dominant page pattern governs the primary path.
- [ ] The opening orients and answers without unnecessary delay.
- [ ] Every must-cover requirement has a section owner.
- [ ] Every section performs a distinct reader job.
- [ ] Sections follow dependency and decision logic.
- [ ] Adjacent intents are summarized and routed, not duplicated.
- [ ] Heading levels are semantic and scannable.
- [ ] Claims and evidence are mapped near each other.
- [ ] Examples are typed and truthfully labeled.
- [ ] Tables, lists, visuals, and interactions have a clear task.
- [ ] Mixed-intent branches are labeled and bounded.
- [ ] Internal links have a defined purpose and destination owner.
- [ ] The CTA matches journey readiness.
- [ ] Mobile, accessibility, and localization needs are documented.
- [ ] Dependencies, blockers, owners, and approvals are visible.
- [ ] Editorial freedom and change-control limits are clear.

## 42. Draft-to-render structural QA

Confirm after drafting and implementation:

- [ ] The final H1 and opening preserve the approved promise.
- [ ] The primary answer is easy to find and remains qualified.
- [ ] The rendered heading outline matches semantic hierarchy.
- [ ] No section was added, removed, or reordered in a way that changes strategy without approval.
- [ ] Section contracts are fulfilled without material duplication.
- [ ] Evidence appears beside the claims it supports.
- [ ] Tables, steps, code, visuals, and callouts work on narrow screens.
- [ ] Interactive and hidden content remains accessible.
- [ ] Anchor links, table of contents, and keyboard navigation work.
- [ ] Internal and external links lead to the intended destinations.
- [ ] FAQs add residual value rather than repeat the body.
- [ ] CTA placement feels earned and offers an honest next step.
- [ ] Localized versions preserve intent while adapting context.
- [ ] Update, version, owner, and freshness signals appear where required.
- [ ] Structured data, if used, matches visible content and eligibility rules.
- [ ] The page is useful when entered at a deep section, not only from the top.

## 44. Final operating rule

Design the page around the smallest complete sequence of answers, evidence, decisions, and actions required by the primary reader.

If a section cannot explain what reader uncertainty it resolves, what task it enables, or what evidence it carries, it does not yet belong in the outline.
