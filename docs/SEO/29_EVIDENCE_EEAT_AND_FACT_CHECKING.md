# Evidence, E-E-A-T and Fact-Checking Standard

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how Neetrino must identify, verify, qualify, cite, approve, publish, maintain, and retire factual claims and trust signals across website content.

Its purpose is to ensure that every material statement is:

- accurate enough for its decision impact;
- supported by evidence appropriate to the claim;
- expressed with justified certainty;
- attributable to a responsible owner;
- traceable after publication;
- current enough for the page's purpose;
- transparent about limitations, assumptions, and conflicts;
- useful to readers rather than merely impressive to algorithms.

This standard governs evidence integrity. It does not promise rankings, AI citations, rich results, authority, or trust merely because a citation, author name, credential, logo, or date is present.

## 2. Scope

Apply this standard to:

- service, industry, solution, comparison, technical, educational, case-study, landing, and resource pages;
- statistics, dates, prices, specifications, benchmarks, performance statements, and market claims;
- legal, financial, security, privacy, accessibility, health, regulatory, and compliance statements;
- customer outcomes, testimonials, awards, certifications, partnerships, client logos, and team credentials;
- first-party research, internal data, experiments, demonstrations, calculators, and interactive tools;
- quoted, paraphrased, translated, summarized, or AI-assisted material;
- structured data and machine-readable claims that represent visible content;
- new publication, optimization, localization, refresh, consolidation, migration, and recovery work.

It applies to visible copy, metadata, captions, tables, charts, downloadable assets, FAQs, forms, CTAs, author information, and supporting interfaces.

## 3. Relationship to other canonical references

Use this document with, not instead of:

- `25_CONTENT_BRIEF_AND_PAGE_PLANNING.md` for evidence planning, owners, risks, and acceptance criteria;
- `26_CONTENT_OUTLINE_AND_INFORMATION_ARCHITECTURE.md` for placing claims and proof within the reader journey;
- `27_CONTENT_WRITING_AND_EDITORIAL_STANDARDS.md` for claim language, attribution, editing, and prose quality;
- `28_ON_PAGE_SEO_AND_CONTENT_OPTIMIZATION.md` for search presentation, links, structured data, and rendered validation;
- brand, service, audience, technical, legal, privacy, security, and measurement references relevant to the page.

If requirements conflict, preserve truth, safety, legality, privacy, accessibility, and reader understanding before promotional or search objectives. Record the conflict and decision.

## 4. Operating principle

Evidence is part of content architecture, not decoration added after drafting.

The required sequence is:

1. identify the claim;
2. classify its type and decision impact;
3. define the evidence required;
4. obtain and assess the evidence;
5. write with calibrated certainty;
6. place attribution where the reader needs it;
7. verify the implemented version;
8. monitor freshness and changed conditions.

Do not publish a strong claim and then search for something vaguely compatible with it.

## 5. Definitions

### Claim

A statement that a reasonable reader could understand as asserting something true, likely, measured, promised, comparative, causal, current, or attributable.

### Material claim

A claim that could affect a reader's purchase, implementation, safety, compliance, reputation, cost, timing, or trust decision.

### Evidence

Information that directly supports, qualifies, or challenges a claim and whose origin, context, method, and limitations can be evaluated.

### Source

The origin from which evidence is obtained. A source is not automatically evidence for every statement it contains.

### Citation

A reader-visible or internal reference that connects a claim to its source. A citation improves traceability; it does not repair weak evidence.

### Verification

The process of determining whether a claim is sufficiently supported for its intended use at the time of review.

### Corroboration

Independent support from another source, dataset, method, or responsible owner.

### First-party evidence

Evidence created from Neetrino's own work, systems, observations, research, or records.

### Third-party evidence

Evidence created outside Neetrino and assessed for independence, competence, relevance, and transparency.

### E-E-A-T

A practical quality lens covering experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust. It is not a numeric score, markup type, or checklist that mechanically produces search performance.

## 6. Non-negotiable rules

- Never invent a source, author, quotation, statistic, customer, project, credential, award, certification, review, result, date, method, or citation.
- Never cite a source that does not support the exact nearby claim.
- Never convert correlation, sequence, opinion, or anecdote into causation.
- Never present estimates, projections, simulations, or hypothetical examples as observed facts.
- Never use client names, logos, results, screenshots, or quotations without documented permission and scope.
- Never claim compliance, certification, partnership, official status, guaranteed outcome, or universal superiority without current proof and authorized wording.
- Never hide material assumptions or limitations where their omission would change the decision.
- Never rely on an AI-generated answer as the evidence source.
- Never publish a mandatory-blocker claim while labeling it “to verify later.”
- Never update a date merely to create a freshness signal.

## 7. Evidence lifecycle

Every material claim moves through these states:

| State | Meaning | Permitted use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PROPOSED | Claim is requested but not assessed | Brief or draft notes only |
| SOURCED | Candidate evidence exists | Drafting with provisional marker |
| VERIFIED | Evidence and wording pass required review | Approved content |
| QUALIFIED | Support exists only with explicit limits | Publish with qualification |
| DISPUTED | Credible evidence conflicts | Publish only with transparent treatment and approval |
| BLOCKED | Evidence is missing, invalid, unauthorized, or unsafe | Do not publish |
| EXPIRED | Evidence exceeded review period or conditions changed | Remove, reverify, or clearly archive |
| RETIRED | Claim is intentionally no longer maintained | Historical record only |

Do not treat SOURCED as VERIFIED.

## 8. Claim inventory requirement

Create a claim inventory before final fact-checking. Include every statement involving:

- numbers, percentages, ranges, dates, durations, volumes, rankings, or trends;
- performance, speed, cost, revenue, conversion, savings, reliability, scale, or efficiency;
- causation, prediction, comparison, superiority, uniqueness, or “best” language;
- technical behavior, compatibility, requirements, limits, vulnerabilities, or specifications;
- legal, policy, privacy, security, regulatory, accessibility, or compliance meaning;
- customer outcomes, team experience, company history, credentials, awards, or affiliations;
- quotations, definitions attributed to authorities, or contested interpretations;
- current availability, price, feature, market, platform, law, personnel, or standard.

Minor common-knowledge statements may be sampled rather than individually logged only when they are low impact, stable, uncontroversial, and unnecessary to the decision.

## 9. Claim classification

Classify each claim before selecting evidence.

| Claim type | Example form | Minimum treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive fact | “The platform supports X” | Current authoritative source |
| Quantitative | “Reduced time by 32%” | Dataset, method, period, denominator |
| Comparative | “Faster than Y” | Equivalent comparison and defined basis |
| Causal | “X caused Y” | Strong design or qualified causal language |
| Forecast | “Demand will grow” | Model/source, horizon, assumptions, uncertainty |
| Recommendation | “Use X when…” | Evidence plus decision criteria and trade-offs |
| Definition | “Term X means…” | Appropriate domain authority and context |
| First-hand observation | “In our implementation…” | Identified experience, scope, and limits |
| Testimonial | Customer quotation | Authentic record, consent, attribution |
| Capability | “Neetrino can deliver X” | Service truth, expertise, capacity, constraints |
| Promise | “You will receive X” | Approved scope, conditions, accountable owner |
| Compliance/status | “Certified/compliant/partner” | Valid documentation and exact authorized wording |

## 10. Decision-impact and risk levels

Assign the highest applicable level:

| Level | Description | Verification requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E0 | Stylistic or subjective, no factual dependency | Editorial review |
| E1 | Low-impact, stable, widely established | One suitable source or accountable internal owner |
| E2 | Material business or technical decision | Strong source, context check, independent review |
| E3 | High-impact financial, security, privacy, legal, regulatory, accessibility, health, or reputational claim | Primary authority, specialist review, explicit approval |
| E4 | Exceptional claim, public controversy, guaranteed outcome, official status, or claim with severe harm potential | Multiple strong sources, authorized expert/legal review, executive approval or rejection |

The claim's wording strength must not exceed the evidence level.

## 11. Source hierarchy

Prefer the closest competent source to the underlying fact:

1. original record, raw dataset, contract, official register, specification, standard, law, filing, product documentation, or direct measurement;
2. authoritative body or responsible organization interpreting its own work;
3. peer-reviewed research, recognized technical authority, or transparent independent analysis;
4. reputable secondary reporting that links to or accurately identifies primary material;
5. qualified practitioner synthesis with disclosed experience and methods;
6. community discussion, forum, or anecdotal report used only for discovery, experience signals, or explicitly labeled examples;
7. search snippets, aggregators, unattributed summaries, generated answers, and copied compilations used only to locate better sources.

Higher placement does not automatically make a source correct. Assess fitness for the precise claim.

## 12. Primary versus secondary sources

Use primary sources when the claim concerns:

- what an organization offers, requires, changed, or officially states;
- laws, regulations, standards, certifications, filings, and official statistics;
- research methods and findings;
- technical specifications, releases, security notices, limits, and compatibility;
- Neetrino's own projects, processes, measurements, and contractual promises.

Use secondary sources to:

- explain context;
- compare interpretations;
- identify limitations or criticism;
- translate specialist material;
- corroborate an event or industry pattern.

Do not cite a commentary about a primary source when the original is accessible and necessary to interpret the claim.

## 13. Source fitness test

Evaluate every material source across:

- authority: who created it and why they are competent;
- proximity: how close it is to the underlying event, data, or decision;
- independence: financial, promotional, institutional, or ideological interests;
- method: how the information was collected, measured, analyzed, or reviewed;
- relevance: whether the population, geography, technology, market, and use case match;
- recency: whether the source is current enough for the claim;
- completeness: whether essential context, definitions, and limitations are available;
- stability: whether the URL, document, dataset, or version can be retrieved later;
- consistency: whether other credible evidence agrees;
- rights and privacy: whether it may be used, quoted, displayed, and retained.

Record material deficiencies rather than hiding them behind the source's brand recognition.

## 14. Source independence and conflicts of interest

Disclose or account for:

- vendor-funded benchmarks;
- affiliate relationships;
- sponsored studies or placements;
- customer or partner incentives;
- Neetrino's commercial interest in a recommendation;
- authors evaluating their own method or product;
- datasets selected by parties benefiting from the conclusion.

A conflicted source can still be useful for specifications or first-party position, but it is weak independent proof of superiority.

## 15. Recency and freshness

Set freshness based on change rate, not a universal age limit.

| Volatility | Examples | Default review approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Very high | prices, personnel, laws, product features, security advisories | Verify near publication and monitor |
| High | platform policies, benchmarks, market data, integrations | Verify during production and scheduled review |
| Medium | process guidance, comparative capabilities, statistics | Review at defined intervals and on material change |
| Low | stable concepts, historical facts, foundational standards | Review when challenged or context changes |

Record “checked on” separately from the source's publication and event dates. A newer article is not necessarily better evidence than the current governing document.

## 16. Version and jurisdiction control

For versioned or jurisdiction-dependent claims, record:

- product, model, release, edition, standard, or API version;
- effective date and superseded status;
- country, region, industry, or contractual jurisdiction;
- environment, configuration, plan, or account tier;
- language version and whether it is authoritative;
- applicability conditions and known exceptions.

Never generalize a regional, plan-specific, experimental, or deprecated rule into a universal statement.

## 17. Quantitative evidence

For every material number, capture:

- metric definition;
- numerator and denominator;
- sample size and population;
- period and comparison window;
- data source and extraction date;
- inclusions, exclusions, and missing data;
- units, currency, tax, inflation, and exchange-rate basis where relevant;
- calculation or transformation method;
- uncertainty, range, or confidence where relevant;
- owner able to reproduce the result.

Avoid false precision. Round consistently and do not turn a directional estimate into a decimal-level fact.

## 18. Percentages, ratios, and relative change

Distinguish clearly between:

- percentage points and percent change;
- relative and absolute change;
- average, median, percentile, and total;
- rate and count;
- gross and net value;
- forecast and observed result;
- cumulative and period-specific performance.

Example: moving from 10% to 12% is a 2-percentage-point increase and a 20% relative increase. Choose the representation that serves understanding, not drama.

## 19. Benchmarks and comparisons

A valid comparison requires:

- the same defined task or outcome;
- comparable populations, environments, periods, and configurations;
- disclosed baselines;
- consistent measurement methods;
- material differences and exclusions;
- a justified comparator;
- language that does not imply universal superiority.

Do not compare Neetrino's best observed result with a competitor's default, outdated, worst-case, or differently measured result.

## 20. Causal claims

Before using “caused,” “led to,” “resulted in,” “improved,” or equivalent language, assess:

- temporal order;
- plausible mechanism;
- alternative explanations;
- selection and survivorship bias;
- seasonality, campaigns, migrations, pricing, staffing, and external events;
- control, counterfactual, experiment, or credible analytical design;
- consistency across evidence.

When causation is not established, use accurate alternatives such as “was associated with,” “coincided with,” “contributed to,” or “the team observed after,” while stating important confounders.

## 21. Forecasts, estimates, and scenarios

Label forecasts and estimates explicitly. Provide:

- responsible author or model;
- creation date and horizon;
- inputs and assumptions;
- scenario name;
- range or uncertainty;
- conditions that would invalidate the estimate;
- distinction from a commitment or guaranteed result.

Do not publish a best-case scenario as “expected” without justification.

## 22. Research and survey claims

Before citing research, review:

- research question;
- study design;
- sample and recruitment;
- population fit;
- field dates;
- definitions and instrument wording;
- funding and conflicts;
- analysis method;
- limitations;
- whether the conclusion is the authors' finding or a secondary interpretation.

Do not infer broad market behavior from a small, self-selected, unrelated, or geographically mismatched sample without qualification.

## 23. Technical claims

Verify technical claims against:

- current official documentation or source code where appropriate;
- exact version and environment;
- reproducible test or implementation record;
- configuration, dependency, and plan constraints;
- deprecation and security status;
- maintainer guidance and known limitations.

Separate documented capability from Neetrino-tested behavior and from a proposed architecture. Code that compiled once is evidence of one state, not an eternal law of nature.

## 24. Security, privacy, and compliance claims

Treat as E3 or E4 when material. Require authorized review for statements involving:

- “secure,” “safe,” “private,” “encrypted,” “anonymous,” or “zero risk”;
- GDPR, CCPA, PCI DSS, HIPAA, ISO, SOC, WCAG, or other regimes and standards;
- data residency, retention, deletion, consent, subprocessors, and breach response;
- penetration testing, vulnerability status, access control, backups, and availability;
- legal rights, obligations, penalties, or eligibility.

Prefer precise control descriptions over broad assurances. Do not use “compliant” when only a feature can support a customer's compliance program.

## 25. High-stakes claims

For legal, financial, medical, safety, security, privacy, or regulated decisions:

- use current primary authorities;
- define jurisdiction, audience, and applicability;
- require qualified specialist review;
- distinguish general information from individualized advice;
- include necessary warnings and escalation paths;
- avoid overconfident summaries of contested or changing rules;
- set a short review interval and event trigger.

A disclaimer does not legalize an inaccurate claim.

## 26. First-party experience evidence

Experience evidence may include:

- direct project participation;
- observed implementation constraints;
- original screenshots, tests, measurements, logs, or artifacts;
- documented lessons from support, delivery, research, or operations;
- first-person account from an identifiable responsible contributor.

State the context: what was done, by whom, when, under which conditions, and what cannot be generalized.

## 27. Case-study evidence

Every material case study must have:

- verified client and project identity or an approved anonymization rationale;
- consent for names, logos, quotations, screenshots, and results;
- baseline and outcome definitions;
- dates and measurement period;
- data source and responsible owner;
- Neetrino's actual contribution;
- other contributors and confounders;
- calculation method;
- limitations and non-guarantee language;
- approval record and expiry/review condition.

Do not imply sole causation when the client, another vendor, seasonality, media spend, pricing, or product changes materially contributed.

## 28. Testimonials, reviews, and quotations

Verify:

- the speaker's identity and relationship;
- the original wording or authorized edit;
- date and relevant context;
- consent for the exact placement, channels, attribution, and duration;
- whether incentives were provided;
- whether the quotation represents typical experience or an exceptional case.

Use ellipses and edits without changing meaning. Never manufacture conversational polish and present it as a verbatim quote.

## 29. Credentials, awards, partnerships, and logos

Before publication confirm:

- exact official name and status;
- granting or partner organization;
- validity period;
- individual versus company scope;
- level, geography, product, or program limitations;
- trademark and logo-use permission;
- required wording or link;
- conditions for removal.

Do not convert participation, listing, training, membership, or tool usage into certification or official partnership.

## 30. Authorship and accountability

Assign authorship based on real contribution, not prestige. An author or reviewer record should state, where appropriate:

- name and role;
- contribution to the content;
- relevant experience or expertise;
- organization and conflicts;
- review responsibility;
- publication and last substantive review dates;
- contact, profile, or correction route.

Do not add a nominal expert who did not review the material.

## 31. E-E-A-T evidence model

### Experience

Demonstrate direct involvement through specific context, process, artifacts, observations, and limitations.

### Expertise

Demonstrate relevant knowledge through accurate reasoning, qualifications where material, technical depth, and appropriate specialist review.

### Authoritativeness

Build recognition through sustained original work, independent references, credible partnerships, transparent authorship, and consistent subject ownership.

### Trust

Prioritize accuracy, source transparency, honest limitations, security, privacy, customer clarity, corrections, ownership, and consistent behavior.

Trust governs the other dimensions. Experience or credentials cannot compensate for deceptive presentation.

## 32. E-E-A-T by page type

| Page type | Priority proof |
| --- | --- |
| Service | capability, process, team, scope, constraints, relevant work |
| Technical | versions, tests, implementation experience, sources, reviewer expertise |
| Case study | consent, baseline, method, contribution, outcome, limitations |
| Comparison | transparent criteria, equivalent evidence, conflicts, update date |
| Industry | domain contributors, regulations, terminology, relevant examples |
| How-to | tested steps, prerequisites, screenshots, failure modes, update version |
| Opinion/insight | author experience, reasoning chain, distinction from fact |
| Tool/calculator | formula, inputs, assumptions, validation, owner, disclaimer |

Do not force the same author bio, citation density, or proof format onto every page.

## 33. Citation requirement

Cite when a claim is:

- material and not established by visible first-party evidence;
- quantitative, comparative, causal, contested, surprising, or time-sensitive;
- derived from another person's research, wording, framework, or data;
- high-stakes or likely to be relied upon;
- an exact quotation or close paraphrase;
- dependent on a specific version, jurisdiction, or policy.

Common knowledge does not require ceremonial citation. Material decisions do.

## 34. Citation placement and granularity

- Place the citation immediately after the claim or smallest claim group it supports.
- Use separate citations when one sentence contains claims supported by different sources.
- Do not attach one citation to a paragraph containing unsupported additions.
- Link to the most direct stable source, not a search results page.
- Identify document title, organization, date, version, page, table, section, or dataset where useful.
- Preserve reader access where possible; disclose paywalls or restricted evidence when material.
- Use footnotes or endnotes only when inline presentation would materially harm readability.

Citation density must follow claim density, not a decorative target.

## 35. Quotations and paraphrases

- Verify quotations against the original.
- Preserve material meaning and context.
- Keep quotations as short as necessary.
- Use quotation marks only for exact language.
- Attribute translations and label them when ambiguity matters.
- Do not patch together fragments into a statement the source never made.
- Cite paraphrases when the idea, data, or analysis belongs to the source.
- Respect copyright, license, and contractual restrictions.

## 36. Tables, charts, images, and screenshots

Every evidence-bearing visual must include or retain:

- source and collection date;
- metric and unit definitions;
- labels, scales, legends, and denominators;
- transformations or calculations;
- sample and time period where relevant;
- accessibility equivalent;
- rights and privacy status;
- warning when illustrative, simulated, cropped, redacted, or not to scale.

Do not use a visually precise chart to imply precision absent from the data.

## 37. Internal data and confidential evidence

Internal evidence must still be reviewable by an authorized owner. Record:

- system of record;
- query, report, or retrieval method;
- date range and extraction date;
- field definitions and filters;
- responsible data owner;
- privacy classification;
- permitted public aggregation;
- reproducibility and retention location.

If evidence cannot be made public, publish only a claim whose wording and internal verification are approved. “Internal data” is not a universal exemption from scrutiny.

## 38. Personal data, confidentiality, and redaction

- Minimize personal and confidential information.
- Obtain documented authorization before public use.
- Redact identifiers consistently across text, images, logs, URLs, and metadata.
- Do not expose credentials, tokens, customer records, private communications, or proprietary architecture.
- Verify that anonymization cannot be trivially reversed through context.
- Retain unredacted proof only in approved systems with access controls.

Privacy review can block publication even when the claim is true.

## 39. Contradictory evidence

When credible sources disagree:

1. confirm they address the same claim, definitions, period, population, version, and jurisdiction;
2. evaluate source proximity, method, conflicts, and recency;
3. seek the governing or original evidence;
4. record the disagreement;
5. present uncertainty or competing interpretations when decision-relevant;
6. escalate rather than selecting the most convenient conclusion.

Do not manufacture consensus or “balance” a strong evidence base with an unsupported fringe claim.

## 40. Missing or insufficient evidence

Choose one of these actions:

- remove the claim;
- narrow its scope;
- reduce certainty;
- label it as hypothesis, estimate, opinion, or observation;
- replace it with a verifiable process statement;
- obtain stronger evidence;
- postpone publication;
- escalate for specialist review.

Do not solve missing evidence by adding vague language that still leaves the same impression.

## 41. AI-assisted research and drafting

AI may assist with:

- creating a provisional claim inventory;
- generating source-search terms;
- comparing definitions or identifying possible conflicts;
- extracting candidate facts from provided material;
- formatting citation records;
- flagging unsupported certainty, stale dates, or inconsistent numbers;
- creating verification checklists.

AI must not be treated as:

- the factual source;
- proof that a source exists;
- an authority on current or high-stakes information;
- confirmation that a citation supports a claim;
- permission to quote, use a logo, or disclose data;
- final approval for technical, legal, financial, security, privacy, or compliance claims.

Every AI-suggested fact and citation must be checked against the actual source.

## 42. Fact-checking workflow

### Stage 1 — Extract

Mark all explicit and implied claims, including headings, captions, metadata, CTAs, tables, charts, and structured data.

### Stage 2 — Classify

Assign claim type, impact level, volatility, jurisdiction, page role, and accountable owner.

### Stage 3 — Map evidence

Connect each claim to direct evidence, required corroboration, permissions, and specialist review.

### Stage 4 — Inspect sources

Open and assess the complete relevant source. Do not validate from a snippet, summary, or citation list alone.

### Stage 5 — Recalculate

Reproduce material calculations, conversions, percentages, ranges, and comparison logic.

### Stage 6 — Calibrate wording

Align scope, tense, certainty, attribution, and limitations with the evidence.

### Stage 7 — Independent review

Have a reviewer other than the writer check E2–E4 claims and any area with meaningful conflict of interest.

### Stage 8 — Approve

Obtain domain, legal, privacy, security, client, brand, or executive approval where required.

### Stage 9 — Verify implementation

Confirm the rendered page preserves approved claims, citations, caveats, links, dates, and structured data.

### Stage 10 — Maintain

Monitor expiry dates, broken sources, changed facts, corrections, and evidence-triggered review conditions.

## 43. Independent reviewer responsibilities

The reviewer must:

- inspect the actual evidence;
- challenge the strongest interpretation;
- verify calculations and quoted wording;
- identify missing context and alternative explanations;
- check version, date, jurisdiction, and applicability;
- confirm permissions and disclosure needs;
- record unresolved issues without rewriting history;
- reject claims whose persuasive effect exceeds their proof.

Approval is not a rubber stamp and seniority is not a substitute for method.

## 44. Material-change control

Reverification is required when a change affects:

- meaning, scope, certainty, number, date, comparison, or conclusion;
- source, link destination, dataset, calculation, or visual;
- client identity, attribution, quotation, consent, or logo;
- author, reviewer, credential, award, certification, or partnership;
- law, policy, standard, feature, product, price, or jurisdiction;
- translation of a material claim;
- structured data or metadata expressing a factual promise.

Cosmetic edits that do not alter meaning may retain approval. Record the basis.

## 45. Corrections and transparency

Provide a correction route appropriate to the publication. When an error is confirmed:

1. assess reader harm and propagation;
2. correct or remove the claim promptly;
3. update dependent metadata, visuals, downloads, schema, and localized versions;
4. notify affected owners or customers when necessary;
5. add a visible correction note when the prior error was material or widely relied upon;
6. preserve an internal audit record;
7. identify and repair the process failure.

Do not silently rewrite a material historical claim in a way that conceals the original error.

## 46. Localization and multilingual evidence

For Armenian, English, and Russian versions:

- preserve semantic scope and certainty, not just words;
- use sources valid for the target market and jurisdiction;
- verify local units, currency, dates, legal terminology, and official names;
- distinguish translated evidence from locally applicable evidence;
- link to an authoritative local-language source where available and equivalent;
- retain caveats, attribution, consent restrictions, and correction notes;
- assign a reviewer competent in both language and subject matter for material claims.

Do not copy a global statistic or regulation into a local page merely because the translation is fluent.

## 47. Structured data and machine-readable claims

Machine-readable claims must:

- represent visible content accurately;
- use eligible and current properties;
- match names, dates, authors, ratings, prices, availability, and organizations shown to users;
- avoid fabricated reviews, awards, credentials, or relationships;
- update when the visible claim changes;
- pass factual review in addition to syntax validation.

Valid markup can still encode a false statement.

## 51. Evidence quality score

Score each criterion from 0 to 2:

- 0 = absent, invalid, or materially misleading;
- 1 = partial, uncertain, or needs remediation;
- 2 = complete and fit for purpose.

Criteria:

1. claim inventory completeness;
2. claim classification accuracy;
3. evidence-to-claim directness;
4. source authority and proximity;
5. method transparency;
6. relevance to population, environment, and task;
7. currency and version control;
8. jurisdiction and applicability;
9. quantitative reproducibility;
10. comparison fairness;
11. causal and certainty calibration;
12. limitations and conflict disclosure;
13. citation placement and traceability;
14. first-party experience integrity;
15. authorship and reviewer accountability;
16. permissions, privacy, and rights;
17. localization equivalence;
18. rendered and machine-readable consistency;
19. correction and maintenance readiness;
20. independent review and approval completeness.

Maximum score: 40.

Suggested readiness interpretation:

- 36–40: ready if no mandatory-zero criterion exists;
- 30–35: conditionally ready after recorded remediation;
- 22–29: not ready; material evidence gaps remain;
- 0–21: structurally unreliable; rebuild the evidence plan.

A score of 0 in criteria 3, 9, 10, 11, 13, 16, or 20 blocks publication when applicable. A high total never overrides a mandatory zero.

## 53. Blocking conditions

Do not publish when:

- a material claim has no adequate evidence;
- a citation is fabricated, inaccessible to reviewers, or materially mismatched;
- a number cannot be reproduced;
- a customer claim lacks consent;
- a credential, certification, award, partnership, or official status is unverified;
- privacy, confidentiality, copyright, trademark, or contractual rights are unresolved;
- an E3 or E4 claim lacks required specialist approval;
- contradictory evidence could materially change the decision and is unresolved;
- localization changes legal, technical, quantitative, or causal meaning;
- the rendered implementation omits a necessary caveat or attribution;
- structured data asserts facts not present or verified on the page;
- a known material error remains uncorrected.

## 54. Anti-patterns

Reject evidence work that:

- adds citations only after the persuasive copy is fixed;
- uses search snippets as sources;
- cites a homepage for a specific technical or legal claim;
- treats a reputable domain as proof of every statement on it;
- uses ten weak citations to simulate one strong source;
- cites a statistic without its denominator, date, geography, or method;
- copies a competitor's unsupported claim and calls it market evidence;
- references a study only through another marketer's summary;
- hides uncertainty in footnotes while the headline remains absolute;
- labels a sales estimate as research;
- presents one project result as typical or guaranteed;
- uses anonymous customer praise without an authenticity record;
- places logos under “trusted by” when the relationship was only incidental;
- gives an honorary reviewer authorship they did not earn;
- treats an “updated” date as evidence of substantive review;
- assumes a translated citation proves local applicability;
- uses AI-generated citations or quotations without opening the original;
- adds author schema, credentials, badges, or references as cosmetic SEO signals;
- preserves a false claim because it performs well;
- counts citations instead of testing whether they support decisions.

## 55. Pre-publication fact-check checklist

### Claims and risk

- [ ] All explicit and implied material claims are in the inventory.
- [ ] Each claim has a type, impact level, volatility, owner, and status.
- [ ] E3–E4 topics have the required specialist path.
- [ ] Headings, metadata, captions, CTAs, visuals, and schema are included in review.

### Sources and support

- [ ] Each material claim maps to direct, fit-for-purpose evidence.
- [ ] Primary sources are used where required.
- [ ] Full sources, not snippets or generated summaries, were inspected.
- [ ] Source authority, proximity, method, relevance, independence, and recency were assessed.
- [ ] Version, jurisdiction, population, plan, and environment are correct.
- [ ] Contradictory evidence is resolved or transparently represented.

### Numbers and reasoning

- [ ] Material calculations were independently reproduced.
- [ ] Denominators, periods, units, currency, and exclusions are visible or recorded.
- [ ] Percent, percentage-point, rate, average, and total language is correct.
- [ ] Comparisons use equivalent definitions and conditions.
- [ ] Causal, predictive, and certainty language matches the evidence.
- [ ] Estimates, forecasts, scenarios, and hypotheses are labeled.

### Experience, expertise, and authority

- [ ] First-hand experience is real, specific, scoped, and attributable.
- [ ] Authors and reviewers made the claimed contribution.
- [ ] Credentials, awards, partnerships, certifications, and logos are current and permitted.
- [ ] Case studies distinguish Neetrino's contribution from other drivers.
- [ ] Trust signals are relevant to this page rather than decorative.

### Attribution, rights, and privacy

- [ ] Citations sit beside the claims they support.
- [ ] Quotations match the original and preserve context.
- [ ] Customer consent covers the exact use.
- [ ] Copyright, license, trademark, contractual, and privacy requirements pass.
- [ ] Confidential evidence is referenced safely and stored appropriately.

### Language and implementation

- [ ] Qualifications and limitations are prominent enough for the decision.
- [ ] Localized claims preserve meaning and local applicability.
- [ ] Links point to final, stable, authoritative destinations.
- [ ] Visible content, metadata, visuals, downloads, and structured data agree.
- [ ] No blocking condition or mandatory-zero score remains.

### Accountability and maintenance

- [ ] Writer, fact-checker, approver, and evidence owner are recorded.
- [ ] Verification date and review trigger are defined.
- [ ] Dependent pages, locales, assets, and claims are known.
- [ ] A correction route exists.

## 56. Post-publication evidence checklist

- [ ] The rendered claims, citations, dates, caveats, bylines, visuals, and schema match approval.
- [ ] Every source link resolves to the intended document and relevant version.
- [ ] Restricted or internal evidence remains protected.
- [ ] Consent, credential, award, partnership, and certification expiry dates are monitored.
- [ ] High-volatility facts have scheduled or event-triggered checks.
- [ ] Broken links, retracted studies, corrected datasets, policy changes, and superseded documentation are monitored.
- [ ] Material corrections propagate to metadata, visuals, downloads, schema, and localized pages.
- [ ] Reader challenges and correction requests have an owner and response target.
- [ ] Archived pages distinguish historical claims from current guidance.
- [ ] Evidence records preserve the final published wording and verification state.

## 57. Acceptance criteria

Content passes this standard only when:

- all material claims are identifiable and traceable;
- evidence strength matches decision impact and wording strength;
- calculations and comparisons are reproducible;
- E-E-A-T signals represent real experience, expertise, recognition, and accountability;
- citations support exact claims and preserve reader context;
- permissions, privacy, confidentiality, and rights are resolved;
- high-stakes claims have authorized specialist review;
- contradictions, uncertainty, limitations, and conflicts are handled honestly;
- localized and machine-readable versions remain equivalent;
- ownership, corrections, expiry, and maintenance are operational;
- no blocking condition or mandatory-zero criterion remains.

## 58. Final governing rule

Publish only what Neetrino can responsibly explain, substantiate, and maintain.

The strongest content is not the content that sounds most certain. It is the content whose claims, evidence, experience, expertise, attribution, limitations, and accountability remain aligned under scrutiny. When persuasion conflicts with proof, narrow the promise, strengthen the evidence, or do not publish the claim.
